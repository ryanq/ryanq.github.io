---
title: Where does AI fit in church production?
slug: ai-in-church-production
publish_date: 2026-02-15T08:00:00
---

I feel the need to write down some of my thoughts about how AI gets used in
church production and maybe in churches more broadly. With AI, it seems like
we’re both in the early days and already looking back at the same time. Progress
has been so fast that I think a lot of us are left with our heads spinning.

I’ve been working in church production for about five years now. I started on
the front line, running services at one of our campuses and leading the
volunteer team that actually does the work on a Sunday. Now I’m in charge of
production at multiple campuses with a team of directors that report to me and
each lead their own team of volunteers. ￼We probably schedule 50–60 volunteers
on a weekend just in production and have a little over 300 volunteers in total.
A major part of everyone’s job on our team is the care and discipling of our
volunteers. ￼

So, one of the major questions we have to ask ourselves when considering a new
technology is whether that technology is making jobs easier or taking the place
of a person serving the church. As an example, until recently we would use
Ableton to run click and guide for our band. Ableton can also fire MIDI notes
and control ProPresenter. If your service is planned out well enough you could
automate all of your lyrics. And that would ensure perfect timing. But is that
the best use in our situation? 

I’ve seen some fairly technically impressive things that people have built using
AI around the church production space. Here's an abbreviated list of tools built
using AI:

- Recognizing and suggesting names and gain settings for audio inputs
- Mixing audio (either for the room or for broadcast)
- Converting a written service plan to a set of slides
- Listening to what’s being sung or said from stage and advancing slides
  automatically
- Counting attendance using imagery from a set of ceiling-mounted cameras

I’m sure there are more that either haven’t been shared online or haven’t been
created yet. Not all of them aim to remove a person from a job; some of them are
automating tedious parts of the job. The question to us is “where do we draw the
line and choose not to use a technology?”

I’ve been crafting a set of rules for myself on when I will and won’t use AI. I
won’t use it to create something new. I won’t use it to write something in less
time than you’ll spend reading it. I won’t let an AI speak for me.

In writing this I feel like I’m close to another one: **I won’t use AI to
replace a person’s role.** Or maybe it should go further: **I won’t use AI that
impacts someone else without their permission.**

Do AI suggestions for inputs make your audio engineer’s job easier? Yes. Do your
guest services volunteers enjoy counting attendance? Probably not. Those uses
aren’t obviating a volunteer’s role.

Could a computer be trained once to nail timing for lyrics in every situation
faster than you could train all of your volunteers to do it? Certainly. Could AI
make a better mix than the guy who’s been mixing your band for ten years? Maybe.
Could you replace the person making slides for you with an AI? Sure. But what do
we lose when those people aren’t needed any more?

Each of those people hypothetically replaced by AI is one person who may no
longer have an opportunity to serve their church. If you’re trying to automate
away a volunteer position at your church with AI, you should take a step back
and ask what impact that has.

That’s where the line is.
